Objective:

Work Carried Out:

**Scope Reset Through Contradiction Discovery and Honest Goal Split:** The session opened not with code but with a deliberate distrust of the previous handoff that had claimed the Internal Beta was effectively clear against the lighter specification gate. A parallel sweep against the source of truth surfaced a fundamental contradiction: the purpose-built specification and the newest criteria-fix matrix treated the milestone as a development-network soft-launch gated on eleven binary criteria nearly complete, while the master-plan dashboard listed a much heavier G1 gate covering eight on-chain authorization gates, an end-to-end validator-slash drive, and a fourteen-day burn-in. Rather than guess which definition the user meant, the fork was surfaced explicitly, and the answer locked the heavier master-plan G1 definition plus messaging service bring-up, zero carry-forwards across blocking-critical-high in scope, and live-operation authorization surfaced at each moment. The fourteen-day burn-in was wall-clock physics, so the goal was split honestly into a Sprint-GO covering everything that gates the burn-in start and a fourteen-day tail covering the burn-in completing zero-priority-zero, because claiming a sprint completes a fourteen-day soak would have been the kind of architecturally dishonest certification the dispatch loop discipline explicitly warns against.

A persistent risk entry covering a kyc-client fail-open pattern was debunked because the actual client was fail-closed and the named stub-approved method did not exist.

**Single-Node Host Memory-Reclaim Livelock Incident Diagnosis:** Mid-execution the validator host froze and required force-rebooting. A three-agent forensic team was dispatched covering operating-system and kernel layer, K3s and workload layer, and remediation research layer. The verdict was unambiguous and partly self-inflicted: a memory-exhaustion reclaim livelock, not a panic and not even a clean out-of-memory kill, with the kernel out-of-memory killer never firing and that absence itself the proof. Persistent system-activity reports captured the slide between approximately eight ten and eight fifty universal time showing memory rising from thirty-one to ninety-four percent, committed memory peaking at three hundred and thirty-seven percent of sixty-two gigabytes of RAM, the five-hundred-twelve-megabyte swap pinned, run-queue three hundred and twenty, the kernel burning ninety-six percent CPU re-faulting pages from disk at roughly two gigabytes per second until power-cycled. The trigger was identified plainly: a transient build burst running turbo across forty-two packages plus a Postgres testcontainer plus five parallel build agents landed on the standing K3s plus Besu plus Postgres plus observability plus registrar load, and the smoking gun was two orphaned testcontainers left running. The structural root cause was that the host was defenseless against a memory spike because kubelet eviction was disk-only with no memory-available threshold making graceful pod eviction structurally impossible, system-reserved and kube-reserved were unset making host-systemd Besu and the registrar Cockroach invisible to the scheduler, seventeen Prometheus-stack containers had no memory limit, and five-hundred-twelve megabytes of swap on sixty-two gigabytes with overcommit set to one and no systemd-oomd left no safety net.

**Non-Destructive Cluster Recovery and Tier-One Safety Net Application:** Recovery turned on one insight: forty-seven non-running pods divided into two classes where the crash-loop-backoff pods were failing only because CoreDNS itself was down due to a containerd name-reservation race from the unclean reboot, so they could not resolve the Postgres primary. Fixing CoreDNS first allowed the dependent set to self-heal, and deleting the stuck CoreDNS pod let its deployment recreate clean, after which the crash-loop set recovered on its own. The remaining create-container-error pods were cleared in namespace batches behind a memory guard set to abort below eight gigabytes available, with the critical path verified green by Besu producing blocks, every backend pod running, and the projector caught up. The Tier-One safety net was applied with deliberate care through read-only pre-checks first then one gated step at a time: an eight-gigabyte swap file via the disk-duplicate utility on the existing filesystem, swappiness lowered to ten, overcommit untouched because changing it would break the JVM Besu fork and Redis background-save, testcontainers removed, and the filesystem table boot-verified. The systemd memory-oomd daemon was installed and enabled but its active-kill tuning was deferred to a calm box because the oom-control utility could not introspect under recovery load and a process-killing control could not be certified without verification.

**Risk-Tiered Review Board on the Remediation Itself:** Recovering the box was not the same as fixing what let it die, and the user drew that line by requesting a board on the remediation itself rather than on the diagnosis. A risk-tiered three-reviewer board ran covering network-and-security, DevOps, and quality leads, and returned five evidence-grounded corrections that each changed what shipped. First, the api-server egress fix had been aimed at the wrong address: the three crash-looping observability pods were being rejected by kube-router at the node's real api endpoint on six-four-four-three after destination network address translation, not at the Service cluster IP, so the network-policy rule had to allow the node IP slash thirty-two plus the WireGuard mesh slash twenty-four on six-four-four-three, captured as a sixth architecture decision record. Second, the system-reserved memory was over-padded at eighteen gigabytes against Besu's allocated eight-gigabyte heap when real resident-set-size was two-point-eight-four gigabytes for Besu and one-point-two gigabytes for Cockroach, corrected to fourteen gigabytes of deliberate headroom. Third, the immediate plan to set a hard memory-max on Besu was rejected because a nine-gigabyte hard cap would out-of-memory-kill an eight-gigabyte JVM heap the moment it touched its off-heap and native allocations, so soft memory-high cushioning shipped now and the hard ceiling deferred to a maintenance window. Fourth, the oomd drop-in scoped to kubepods would race the kubelet's own eviction logic on the pod control group and was rescoped to user-slice only where the build burst actually lived. Fifth, the observability stack memory bounding had to cover more than just Prometheus.

**Helm Release Failure and Operator-Native Escape Hatch:** Executing the remediation went sideways at one point recorded honestly because journaling exists to capture exactly that kind of moment. Setting observability memory limits the orthodox way through a helm upgrade on the prometheus-kube-stack release failed on a pre-existing defect in the release where the node-exporter service and daemon-set both declared a port named http-metrics which the api server rejected on patch, and the rollback failed identically because the same defect blocked helm in both directions. Worse, the partial upgrade had already stamped a chart-default five-hundred-twelve-mebibyte limit onto the pods, too low for Prometheus. The fix that worked was operator-native: a direct kubectl patch on the Prometheus custom resource set three gigabytes plus an eight-gigabyte retention cap, and the operator rolled the stateful-set cleanly. The pattern worth keeping was that when a helm release carries a pre-existing manifest defect, helm can move neither forward nor back through it, and the operator's own custom resource is the escape hatch. The certification was deliberately not a forced ten-of-ten but ten of eleven safe-now rows green with one row covering the alert firing under synthetic memory pressure deliberately deferred because driving a freshly-recovered host into memory pressure was the wrong risk on the wrong day, plus three additional rows for kubelet reserve and eviction thresholds and the hard memory-max deferred to a scheduled maintenance window.

**Parallel Branch-Synchronization Arc Across Ten Repositories with Concurrent Developer Conflict Resolution:** A concurrent terminal session ran a branch-synchronization arc reconciling the development branch against the secondary cross-cutting branch across ten repositories. The pre-flight discovered six modified submodule pointers and one tracked file in the dirty parent tree, all genuine forward movement covering roughly ninety-five commits of context across the backend-core messaging route alias, infrastructure-ops main-network observability marathon, the-website admin accessibility and integration-test push, and user-wallet credential scrub, plus an accessibility baseline that had introduced a new serious violation worth surfacing in the commit message rather than laundering. Seven file-by-file commits cleaned the tree before any synchronization proceeded. The symmetric divergence on the parent read seventeen ahead and one hundred and seventy-two behind from the remote, meaning the local branch had diverged because a second developer had pushed seventeen commits the local branch had never seen. The synchronization skill assumed local was source of truth and would have produced either a non-fast-forward rejection or, if force-pushed to fix it, would have silently deleted the second developer's commits. Merge was chosen over rebase deliberately because rebase rewrites SHAs and manufactures duplicates while merge preserves both histories and never rewrites a shared commit, a discipline correct for cross-branch cross-developer synchronization.

**Submodule Pointer Conflicts Resolved by Merge-Base Ancestry Proof:** The infrastructure-ops repository surfaced a submodule merge conflict with a could-not-read object error because one side's commit was not in the local object store, and a submodule pointer conflict is not a text conflict so resolving it wrong silently drops whichever developer's submodule commits get discarded. Rather than picking a side, the relationship was proved: the staged-files list with the unmerged flag gave the three stage SHAs for base, ours, and theirs, a full fetch made the could-not-read object appear because it had simply not been fetched, and the merge-base is-ancestor check returned true with theirs being an ancestor of ours and the merge-base itself being the theirs SHA, meaning resolving to ours was provably lossless. The same conflict happened again before the parent push when a re-fetch showed the remote secondary branch had moved during the synchronization with the other developer pushing another pointer bump mid-operation, and this time the same ancestry drill proved the answer was the opposite: theirs was the descendant containing four new runbook commits stacked directly on top of the local commits, so the lossless resolution flipped to fast-forward to theirs. The crystallized rule was that the resolution is never ours-wins or theirs-wins but whichever commit is the descendant proven by merge-base, because the descendant contains both.

**Pre-Push Gate Root-Cause Fix Over Bypass and Workflow Inversion Discovery:** The institutional-admin-center submodule rejected both branch pushes with its pre-push hook reporting two errors on generated next-pwa service-worker files that the TypeScript parser choked on because they were not in any tsconfig project. Three options were made explicit: bypass via the no-verify flag, fix the root cause by adding the generated files to the flat-config global-ignores, or hold the push. The root-cause fix was chosen because routing around a red gate with no-verify is how pre-existing debt stays invisible until expensive, and five lines in the eslint configuration took errors from two to zero. The synchronization completed in two passes, with a background watcher polling the remote every ninety seconds for steady-state before the second pass, and the most important finding hid in a commit subject rather than a conflict: the second developer was installing a session-start hook to make the secondary branch the default branch for every session in this repository, a workflow inversion the entire synchronization skill assumed away. The finding was flagged to the user and recorded as the highest-priority carry-forward because syncing in the wrong direction is a coordination problem no merge strategy can fix.
