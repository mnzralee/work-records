Title: MOD-11 Bridge Closure: Six-Defect Remediation, Four-Track Wave 1 Parallel Dispatch, CQRS Transactionality Enforcement & Diamond Storage Registry Unification

Objective:
To close the six concrete defects D1 through D6 identified in the Partner On-Chain Bridge specification by orchestrating a four-track Wave 1 parallel dispatch (dependency-injection container decomposition, Prisma migrations, Diamond storage registry, and Hardhat coverage uplift) followed by a two-track Wave 2 (three-eyes producer use-cases and atomic outbox transaction enforcement), and to harden the platform's CQRS discipline through a custom ESLint rule that fails the build whenever an outbox write occurs outside a Prisma transaction. The secondary goal was to remediate accumulated continuous-integration debt across the monorepo, dispatch the next session's lifecycle use-cases as Wave 1, and consolidate the bridge-spec, scorecard, supply-chain, and operator-runbook deliverables that frame the module's transition from implementation to mainnet cutover.

Work Carried Out:

**Bridge Defect Surface and Track Decomposition Rationale:** The day opened against a precisely delimited problem statement: the existing partner facet (1,397 lines, fourteen on-chain functions today, nineteen target after the module closes) and the partner-bounded subtree under the organization service had drifted out of alignment in six concrete ways enumerated in the Partner On-Chain Bridge specification. Defect D1 captured a three-eyes flow that had collapsed to two eyes off-chain while the on-chain side enforced three for both slash and settlement. Defect D2 named seven on-chain functions with no outbox handler and no producer at all, including initiate-slash, approve-slash, initiate-settlement, approve-settlement, configure-staking, unstake, and validate-profit-report. Defect D3 surfaced a CQRS atomicity violation in which producer use-cases were writing the read-model row and the outbox row in two separate Prisma calls rather than within one shared transaction, leaving a window in which a crash between the two writes would orphan one or the other. Defect D4 noted that the seven-day pending-action expiry encoded on-chain had no off-chain mirror because the settlement-approval row carried no expiresAt column. Defects D5 and D6 covered ordering and synchronization gaps respectively, sequenced for later sessions but called out for tracking. The orchestration plan decomposed Wave 1 into four parallel tracks chosen so that file-ownership clusters held no overlap: a dependency-injection container decomposition, a Prisma migrations track, a Diamond storage registry interface, and a Hardhat test-suite uplift. Wave 2 was scoped as two parallel tracks (producer use-cases and atomic transaction wrapping) gated on the container decomposition landing first, since both Wave 2 tracks would otherwise compete for the same 874-line dependency-injection file.

**Wave 1 Track 0: Bottom-Up Dependency-Injection Decomposition:** The first track took ownership of decomposing the organization service's container.ts (874 lines of order) into bounded modules so that subsequent waves could work the partner namespace concurrently without merge churn, and decomposing the monolithic partner controller (449 lines) into per-feature controllers. The original plan called for four DI modules along partner, merchant, webhook, and partner-portal axes, but live execution surfaced a hidden coherence boundary: a fifth cluster of bindings covering budget, sub-account, compliance, reporting, and balance wiring formed an org-support cohort that could not be folded into any of the four planned modules without violating Single Responsibility. A bottom-up discovery pass pulled handlers out first and then identified the binding clusters that wired them, producing the fifth org-support module at 115 lines and reducing container.ts to 191 lines, comfortably under the 200-line target. The partner controller was split into partner-lifecycle, partner-staking, and partner-settlement controllers, each owning a coherent slice of the HTTP surface. Eleven commits closed the track with the TypeScript compiler clean and existing test suites preserving zero regressions, validating the principle that bottom-up boundary discovery more reliably finds natural module seams than top-down planning.

**Wave 1 Track A: Prisma Migrations and the Authorised-Admins Snapshot Column:** The second track produced five Prisma migrations (M1 through M5) covering the partner-bridge atomic table, a new slash-approval table, settlement-approval column extensions, the staking-configuration on-chain link, and the profit-report reversal-amount column. Track A also added the authorisedAdminsSnapshot Json column to both the settlement-approval and slash-approval tables, capturing the set of administrators eligible to approve at the moment of initiation rather than at the moment of approval, mitigating the stakeholder-rotation race in which an administrator could be removed between initiating an action and another administrator approving it. The agent generated migration SQL by hand from the schema changes without a live development-network database connection, then validated through Prisma's syntax pass. Six commits brought the track to acceptance with the new slash-approval model carrying fourteen columns (including the snapshot), the settlement-approval model extended with nullable expiresAt and onchainSettlementId columns, and Prisma client regeneration completing cleanly.

**Wave 1 Track D: Diamond Storage Registry Library and Selector-Clash Mitigation:** The third track addressed the latent risk that splitting the partner facet for the next module would produce selector clashes across the twenty facets and fourteen libraries that compose the Diamond proxy (EIP-2535). Eighteen storage-slot constants were scattered across thirteen files, with two libraries each containing two-or-three distinct slot declarations, and no single source of truth enforced uniqueness. The track created the storage-slots interface registry and migrated every existing slot constant to import from it, but live execution discovered a Solidity 0.8.24 language constraint: constant declarations are not allowed inside interfaces. The agent corrected the design to a library, adjusting the visibility modifier from internal to public so that the library would compile correctly. A Hardhat test (diamond-storage-uniqueness.spec.ts) was written to read the constants by name and assert that no two resolved to the same keccak-256 hash, and a continuous-integration gate was added to scan the facets and libraries directories for inline keccak-256 declarations that bypass the registry. Nineteen commits landed with all eighteen existing slots accounted for, no duplicate values, the existing 574-test baseline preserved, and the gate exiting non-zero on a deliberately injected violation before returning to clean.

**Wave 1 Track E: Hardhat Coverage Uplift and Deprecation Linting:** The fourth track expanded the partner-facet Hardhat suite from thirty-five to forty-one passing tests by adding six new test cases that filled coverage gaps called out in Section 9.5 of the bridge specification, and registered a new ESLint rule (no-deprecated-domain-primitives) at warning level so that legacy primitive symbols would surface in code review without failing the build. The track also encountered the cross-track collaboration pattern that validated the parallel-dispatch hypothesis: while Track D was midway through its commit sequence, the visibility modifier on the storage-slots library produced a transient working-tree state that Track E's compile would have failed against, but Track E read the commit log, worked around the conflict by building only the specific tests in its own scope, and completed on schedule while Track D committed its visibility fix as part of its own logical sequence. Three commits across the test suite, the lint rule, and a parent-repository documentation update closed the track.

**Wave 2 Track B: Three-Eyes Producer Use-Cases and Twelve Handlers:** With Track 0 landed, Wave 2 dispatched the producer use-cases that close defects D1 and D2. Track B implemented seven new producer use-cases covering the complete three-eyes flow (initiate, approve, and execute for both slash and settlement), three further use-cases for unstake, validate-profit-report, and configure-staking, seven new outbox command handlers in the outbox-submitter worker, and five new projector handlers in the projector worker. Each handler followed the established CQRS pattern with a resolveChaincode method mapping the command type to the correct contract-name, function-name, and arguments tuple, an enrichPayload method running final database lookups to fetch the captured authorised-admins snapshot, and a postCommitSideEffects method updating read models after on-chain confirmation. The projector handlers preserved idempotency-first discipline by checking transaction-already-processed status before touching the database, wrapping all database mutations inside a Prisma transaction, and running notification side effects (alerting webhooks, administrator emails, observability breadcrumbs) outside the transaction with explicit catch blocks. Four new domain-typed errors were introduced: three-eyes-violation, pending-action-expired, slash-approval-not-found, and missing-fabric-partner-identifier. Twenty-eight commits across use-cases, handlers, projectors, and domain errors closed the track with all twenty-six partner-facet functions covered by a producer.

**Wave 2 Track C: Atomic Outbox Transaction Enforcement and Custom ESLint Rule:** Track C addressed defect D3 by sweeping all twenty-three production producer files (those writing outbox rows alongside partner-namespace read models) into Prisma transaction callbacks, using the optional-transaction-client parameter pattern in which repositories accept an optional transaction handle and resolve to the shared client when none is provided. Seventy percent of producers already followed this pattern; the track swept the remaining thirty percent. More consequentially, the track shipped a custom ESLint rule (outbox-create-must-be-transactional) that walks the abstract syntax tree of every file under the partner, organization, and stakeholder use-case subtrees to detect outbox-create invocations occurring outside a Prisma transaction callback, registered at error level so the build fails on any violation. Test-file updates introduced a make-mock-prisma factory function and held one hundred seventy-four tests passing without regression. Twenty-three commits closed the track, and the new rule immediately proved its worth by flagging a backward-compatibility shim that Track B had left in place, demonstrating that domain-specific custom-rule enforcement catches violations that static analysis alone cannot surface.

**Cleanup Sweep, Arity Defect, and Cross-Track Audit Discoveries:** A cleanup agent then drove a focused remediation sequence after both Wave 2 tracks reported completion. The verification gate flagged two ESLint violations from the legacy single-step initiate use-cases that Track B had retained as backward-compatible exports, and a parallel partner-facet ABI audit surfaced a pre-existing arity defect in the execute-slash command handler where a spurious second argument was being passed to a one-argument on-chain function. The cleanup agent replaced the legacy use-case constructors in the controllers with the new three-eyes producers, decomposed schema definitions into typed validation schemas with body-shape, enum-guard, and amount-positive validation, wired six new HTTP routes (initiate, approve, and execute for both slash and settlement) that had been defined but never registered, deleted the backward-compatibility shims to force compile-time enforcement of the three-eyes flow, and corrected the arity defect by removing the spurious argument from both the chaincode-resolver and the contract-resolver. The cross-track audit also caught a missing route registration discovered by the next-session dispatch-prep agent, validating the orchestration assumption that parallel governance (audit, dispatch-prep, verification script) compresses the feedback loop from review-phase to implementation-phase.

**Continuous-Integration Debt Sweep and the Three-Iteration Migration Saga:** The session's perfection-pass mission identified eight categories of accumulated continuous-integration debt: a substantive multi-iteration migration saga over a duplicate type-creation collision, a Node version compatibility issue surfaced by peer-dependency ranges that excluded Node 18, a register-partner command handler missing a method from the abstract base contract, a Software Bill of Materials scan failing because a list command exits non-zero on extraneous packages while still emitting valid JSON, a core-domain test file carrying eight TypeScript errors caused by API drift in a Result value-object, and five orthogonal import-order violations scattered across the core-domain, core-openapi, and core-database packages. The most instructive defect was the migration saga: a first attempt to delete the duplicate-type migration exposed a downstream gap because three types it created were still referenced by schema models; a second attempt to bridge the gap created a fresh collision because a later module-five migration also declared five of the same types; a third attempt finally trimmed the bridge to only the three types missing from both predecessors, using an idempotent exception block to skip creation if a type already existed. The lesson was that local file-level reasoning ("byte-for-byte identical, safe to delete") is not sufficient when migration sequences carry transitive dependencies, and the correct verification pattern requires inspecting the schema for type references and every migration in the directory before promoting any deletion.

**Session 115 Wave 1 Dispatch with Verification-First Discipline:** Once the perfection pass reached zero blockers, the next session's Wave 1 dispatched five tracks in parallel covering lifecycle use-cases, HTTP routes, three-eyes snapshot enforcement, validator dashboard skeletons, and partner admin center scaffold. Track A discovered through a verification-first audit that all seven targeted lifecycle use-cases already existed in the partner subtree from earlier work, refused to duplicate the implementation, and instead swept eleven import-order violations across the existing files. Track B confirmed by the same discipline that all seven HTTP routes were already wired by the prior controller decomposition. Track C delivered substantive new infrastructure: an admin-snapshot service exposing a capture-authorised-admins method and an assert-caller-in-snapshot method, wired into the approve-application use-case with a new stakeholder-rotation-violation domain error and an authorised-admins-snapshot column on the partner-application schema, closing the snapshot pattern (capture-at-initiation, verify-on-action) that prevents administrator-rotation races across all three-eyes flows. Track D contributed lean validator dashboard skeleton pages with sidebar navigation, deliberately mock-data only and pending API design. Track E created a new top-level Next.js 16 partner-admin-center application at 170 files (54 page routes, 53 error boundaries, 27 layouts, plus configuration), establishing the route groups (public, auth, main) and complete folder skeleton that subsequent sessions would populate.

**MOD-11 Continuum: Profit-Share Visualization, Hub Context Switching, Loans, and Audit-Log Hardening:** The MOD-11 implementation continuum that the day's bridge closure unlocked moved through subsequent capability layers worth recording for narrative completeness. A profit-share visualization surface (list, detail, kanban queue, settlement-actions component) operationalized the three-eyes constraint in the operator dashboard with status-coded badges, countdown timers refreshing every sixty seconds, and disabled-state tooltips that surface the rule ("caller must differ from initiator") before a click rather than relying on chain reverts. The Hub MOD-6 context-switching service was extended with a partner-admin context type alongside the existing government and institution types, gated behind a phase-one stub that returned null to keep the partner tile invisible until the data-layer wiring completed in phase two, with tier-based gates (starter, growth, scale, enterprise) and JTI-denylist integration mitigating session-fixation risk. The partner admin center Phase C credential management screens introduced a display-once banner pattern for newly created and rotated keys (the secret never persists in component state beyond the immediate render) and a three-gate revoke confirmation pattern (reason textarea, acknowledgment checkbox, type-the-exact-prefix input) making accidental deletion impossible. Validator domain entities, a seven-day bond timelock with atomic lock-before-event ordering, reward distribution on a 24-hour scheduler, and three-eyes slashing approval rounded out the validator track. Personal-loan and business-loan use-cases introduced trust-score-driven five-tier gating and asymmetric profit-loss sharing economics, and an append-only audit-log hardened by a Postgres trigger raising an unconditional exception on update or delete shifted immutability enforcement from application logic to the database layer (the F-6 mitigation), where mutations become cryptographically impossible rather than merely policy-prohibited.

**Supply-Chain Hardening, Final Scorecard, and Operator Handoff:** The closing tracks moved the module from implementation completeness to operator handoff. A Software Development Kit publish pipeline declared three SDKs (Node, Python, Go) with a custom 340-line Zod-to-OpenAPI converter generating a 3,140-line OpenAPI 3.1 specification covering thirty-six paths and thirty-six schemas, chosen over an external dependency because the schema surface was small and a purpose-built converter avoided the noise of a general library. SLSA Level 3 supply-chain security wired cosign keyless signing (anchored to GitHub OIDC) for sixteen container images plus the SDK artifacts, syft for CycloneDX SBOM generation, and a Kyverno admission policy enforcing signed-image requirements cluster-wide in audit mode initially. A final scorecard agent walked the ten-pillar verification grid and produced a 458-line evidence document scoring 91 out of 100 (the deficit covering deployment-gate items rather than code-quality gaps), accompanied by thirteen mainnet-cutover-gate carry-forward items breaking down into prerequisites-before-cluster-operations, operator-executable-with-agent-prep, and genuine-operator-only categories. A 717-line operator runbook covering nine actions with sixty-eight distinct commands and full rollback procedures, a 1,132-line partner-onboarding playbook covering nine lifecycle stages plus a ten-stage validator track, a twenty-row risk register, and four compliance documents (PCI-DSS 4.0, SOC 2 Type II, GDPR-EAA portability, EU Data Act minimization) closed the documentation surface.
